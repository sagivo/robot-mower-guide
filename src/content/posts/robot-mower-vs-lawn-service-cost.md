## What a lawn service actually costs

Weekly mowing service in most US suburbs runs **$40–$80 per visit** in 2026. A 30-week season (roughly April through October, varies by climate) therefore costs:

| Service level | Per visit | Per season (~30 visits) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic mow, budget market | $40 | $1,200 |
| Typical suburban | $55 | $1,650 |
| Mow + edge + cleanup, premium market | $80 | $2,400 |

And that's the recurring part. Service prices rise most years. A robot mower's price is paid once.

Note the hidden costs of the service model: you pay whether or not the grass needed cutting that week, you're locked to their schedule, and rainy-week backlogs mean your lawn gets mowed two weeks' worth of growth at once (bad for the turf).

### Year 1–5 ownership costs

**Electricity.** Robot mowers sip power. A typical season costs roughly **$20–$40 in electricity**. This is not a typo — they're low-wattage devices that charge slowly between short mowing sessions.

**Blades.** The little razor blades need replacing 2–4 times a season. A year's supply runs **$20–$40**. They're a 5-minute swap, no tools beyond what's in the box.

**Battery.** The one real wear item. Expect 2–4 years per battery depending on lawn size and climate; a replacement runs roughly $100–$300 depending on the model. Budget one replacement in a 5-year window for a large lawn, zero for a small one.

**Repairs.** Under warranty (typically 2–3 years) this is the manufacturer's problem. After that, most owners report near-zero repair costs — there are no engines, belts, or spark plugs to fail.

## Costs people forget to count (both sides)

**In the robot mower's favor:**
- **Your time.** A push-mow takes 1–2 hours weekly; at any reasonable valuation of your weekend, that's worth more than the mower.
- **Lawn health.** Robot mowers cut a little every day and mulch the clippings back in. Frequent light cutting is genuinely better for turf than weekly scalping — agronomists have said so for decades. You may spend less on fertilizer and overseeding.
- **No scheduling.** It mows in the rain (light rain), at night, while you're on vacation.

**In the lawn service's favor:**
- **Zero effort.** A robot mower still needs blade changes, occasional cleaning, and winter storage. A service needs you to open the gate.
- **Edging and cleanup.** Robot mowers don't edge, trim around obstacles precisely, or blow clippings off the driveway. Budget a string trimmer and 20 minutes a month, or a periodic cleanup visit.
- **Theft and damage risk.** A $2,500 machine lives outside. GPS tracking and PIN locks are standard on flagships — confirm yours has them.
- **Upfront cash.** $2,500 today vs. $55 a week is a real liquidity difference.

## Robot mower vs. doing it yourself

The comparison above assumes you'd otherwise pay for service. But many buyers are choosing between a robot mower and mowing it themselves. That math is different — and more personal.

A riding mower costs $1,500–$3,000 plus fuel, oil, blades, belts, and winterization — realistically $200–$400/year in running costs. A push mower is $300–$600 with minimal upkeep. Neither is free in time: figure 1–2 hours weekly for an average suburban lawn, more in spring.

So the DIY comparison isn't about money — it's about time. At 40 hours a season, even valuing your weekend time modestly makes the robot mower's payback obvious. The honest question: do you enjoy mowing? Plenty of people do. If mowing is your Saturday meditation, keep the push mower. If it's a chore you dread, the robot wins on quality of life alone, and the money math is a bonus.

One more angle: physical ability. Robot mowers are genuinely life-changing for owners with back problems, heat sensitivity, or mobility limits. No affiliate site says this enough — it's one of the strongest real reasons people buy.

## The lawn-health dividend (it's real)

This gets dismissed as marketing, but the agronomy is sound:

- **Frequent light cutting** (the robot's daily micro-trim) stresses grass less than weekly scalping. Grass cut by a third of its height recovers faster than grass cut in half.
- **Mulched clippings** return nitrogen to the soil. Over a season, this measurably reduces fertilizer needs — some turf studies suggest up to 25% less nitrogen input.
- **No heavy equipment ruts.** A 30-lb robot doesn't compact soil the way a 500-lb riding mower does. Less compaction means better root growth and drainage.

None of this shows up in a 5-year cost table, but owners consistently report thicker lawns within one season. If you currently pay for fertilization or overseeding, factor in potential savings — conservatively, $100–$300/year for a typical lawn.

## Resale and longevity: what happens in year 6+

Robot mowers hold value surprisingly well. A 3-year-old flagship in working order typically resells for 40–60% of its purchase price — there's an active secondhand market because the tech improves slowly enough that last-gen models are still excellent.

Lifespan expectations from long-term owners: 5–10 years for quality machines. The chassis, motors, and electronics outlast the battery (2–4 years, replaceable) and the blades (consumable). The main killer is neglect — mowers left outside through freezing winters with dead batteries don't come back.

Practical implication for the math: even at year 5, your $2,500 mower is worth ~$800–$1,000 used. The true 5-year cost of ownership is closer to $2,000, not $3,050. The table above is conservative.
